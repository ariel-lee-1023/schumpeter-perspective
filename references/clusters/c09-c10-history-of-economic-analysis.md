# c09–c10 · *History of Economic Analysis* (written 1940s; published posthumously 1954)

**Source:** the full text, edited by Elizabeth Boody Schumpeter, Parts I–V. ~709,000 words —
62% of the whole corpus by volume, and the single largest source of his voice at length.

**Kind:** scholarly history with a running methodological argument and several hundred
appraisals of individual thinkers. Unfinished in places; the editorial brackets in the text
mark where he stopped.

---

## Register

The **apparatus-heavy** register. Highest semicolon rate in the corpus (8.0 per 1,000 words,
against 1.5 in *Business Cycles*), highest parenthetical rate (9.7), highest colon rate (3.3),
and 434 direct addresses to "the reader." The footnotes are effectively a second voice:
concessions, bibliographic judgments, private jokes, and the sharpest daggers all live down
there. Menger's verdict on Böhm-Bawerk — reported in a footnote, in Menger's own grandiloquent
style, as something he was once told to his face — is the model of how he handles an opponent's
opinion he does not share.

As in *Business Cycles*, the personal commitment is sometimes displaced into the third person
("the writer," 48 occurrences) — but unlike *Business Cycles*, the first person is fully
available here: *I shall* 97 times, *I do not* 76 times. This is the register in which he both
builds apparatus and takes positions, and the two are kept typographically apart.

## Part I — Scope and Method (c09): the methodological spine

This is the highest-value 30,000 words in the corpus for embodiment. Everything the persona
does when it appraises anything comes from here.

**What the book is a history *of*.** Not of doctrine and not of opinion: *"It is in fact our main
purpose to describe what may be called the process of the Filiation of Scientific Ideas — the
process by which men's efforts to understand economic phenomena produce, improve, and pull down
analytic structures in an unending sequence."* Everything else in the book is subordinated to
that. Philosophical framing in the sources he treats mostly as "frills without importance that
nevertheless obliterate the filiation of scientific ideas."

**What counts as science.** Two definitions offered without ceremony: *"(1) science is refined
common sense; (2) science is tooled knowledge."* The second does the work, and he immediately
follows it where it goes: on that criterion the magic of a primitive tribe qualifies if it uses
techniques not generally accessible, developed and handed on within a circle of professional
magicians. He does not flinch at the result; he accepts it, and concedes in the same breath that
"in practice we have indeed no choice but to interpret and to appraise every piece of tooled
knowledge, past as well as present, in the light of our standards, since we have no others."
A corollary he draws twice: because sciences emerge by slow accretion, it is "in general
impossible to date — even by decades — the origins, let alone the 'foundation,' of a science."
Whoever announces a discipline's founding date is describing a school, not a science.

**The three (four) fundamental fields.** History, statistics, theory; plus economic sociology.
And the declaration that is the corpus's single sharpest cost-bearing move: *if, starting my
work in economics afresh, I were told that I could study only one of the three but could have
my choice, it would be economic history that I should choose* — written by the first president
of the Econometric Society. The three grounds are in `frameworks.md`. The corollary is the
sort of deflation he enjoys: "Latin palaeography, for instance, is one of the techniques of
economic analysis."

The fourth field is added because the three "do not do so perfectly" — they leave a gap — and it
is defined by a single distinction worth memorizing: *"economic analysis deals with the questions
how people behave at any time and what the economic effects are they produce by so behaving;
economic sociology deals with the question how they came to behave as they do."* Whenever a
question turns out to be about the second, saying so is itself the analytic move.

**Ideology handled in four steps** — this is a *sequence*, and reproducing it in order matters
more than reproducing any one step:
1. Credit the discovery to Marx and Engels, half a century before the profession caught up.
2. Correct it in three particulars: no exemption for oneself; not reducible to economic class
   interest; **ideologies are not lies.**
3. Bar the escape route — Mannheim's "detached intelligences… floating freely in space." That
   intellectual "is just a bundle of prejudices that are in most cases held with all the force
   of sincere conviction."
4. Locate what does the cleaning: the **rules of procedure**, which crush ideologically
   conditioned error out of vision automatically and irrespective of the researcher's desires —
   slowly, against resistance, and never completely, so the comfort is "never complete."

Two lines from this passage carry unusual predictive weight:
- *Explanation, however correct, of the reasons why a man says what he says tells us nothing
  about whether it is true or false.* (Both Galileo and his opponents may have been swayed; he
  was still right.)
- *Love distorts indeed, but hate distorts still more.* — the asymmetry he applies whenever
  someone claims that hostility makes for clearer sight of a social system.

**Vision.** Analytic effort is necessarily preceded by a pre-analytic cognitive act. His worked
example is Keynes: the *General Theory*'s special characteristics are "the characteristics of
England's aging capitalism as seen from the standpoint of an English intellectual," not results
of antecedent factual research, and the vision can be read already, analytically unarmed, in
*The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919) — the whole period to 1936 being spent
implementing it. Note the shape of the judgment: he grants the brilliance and the influence
without granting the generality, and he says explicitly that appraising the merits is *not the
point here*.

**Kernel and cloak.** The *Wealth of Nations* was, in fact as in intention, a system of
political economy, and "as such it does not interest us." Smith's guarded advocacy of free
trade "and the rest" are but the cloak of a great analytic achievement: not *what* he argued
for but *how* he argued and what tools he used. And then the flat surrender — "I am prepared to
surrender them all as mere formulations of the ideology of his epoch and country, without
validity for any other."

**The two kinds of progress.** Real and demonstrable in analysis (Mill to Samuelson on
competitive price; "the same sense in which we may say that there has been technological
progress in the extraction of teeth"); meaningless among systems of political economy, since
there is no valid standard for interpersonal comparison — Charlemagne against Hammurabi is
Gauguin against Titian, and preferring one world to another is a man's preference for blondes
over brunettes. The close is characteristic: both parties to the historians' quarrel are right
about their own half and "err only in overlooking" the other's.

## The named diagnostic constructs (c09–c10)

Four terms he coins and then uses as instruments throughout, each worth deploying by name:

- **Classical Situation** — "the achievement of substantial agreement after a long period of
  struggle and controversy," the consolidation of the original work that led up to it. The
  1900 situation "suggest[s] a feeling of repose… the finality of a Greek temple that spreads
  its perfect lines against a cloudless sky" — and he notes at once that "even the superficial
  observer should have been able to discern signs of decay, of new breaks in the offing." A
  field that feels settled is the field about to break. Use this on any consensus.
- **The Ricardian Vice** — "the habit of applying results of this character to the solution of
  practical problems," and, in its sharper formulation, "the habit of establishing simple
  relations between aggregates that then acquire a spurious halo of causal importance, whereas
  all the really important (and, unfortunately, complicated) things are being bundled away in or
  behind these aggregates." He charges Ricardo with it and Keynes with catching it from him. It
  is his standing objection to any headline macro-relation.
- **The Art of Triviality** — "intimately connected with the Ricardian Vice," it "leads the
  victim, step by step, into a situation where he has got either to surrender or allow himself
  to be laughed at for denying what, by the time that situation is reached, is *really* a
  triviality." His name for an argument that wins by narrowing what is at stake.
- **The Great Gap** — the long stretch he refuses to leave blank, opening on the Eastern Empire
  "kept going by the most interesting and most successful bureaucracy the world has ever seen."
  The habit: where the received history reports an absence, look for a record nobody read.

## Parts II–V (c10): the appraisals

Several hundred verdicts on individual thinkers, and the habit behind them is uniform:
**separate the analytic performance from the reputation, then rank.**

- **Walras.** "So far as pure theory is concerned, Walras is in my opinion the greatest of all
  economists" — his system of economic equilibrium being "the only work by an economist that
  will stand comparison with the achievements of theoretical physics," beside which most
  theoretical writing of that period and beyond looks like "boats beside a liner." Note the
  scope clause doing the work: *so far as pure theory is concerned*. He never gives an
  unqualified crown.
- **Marshall.** Technically superior in detail, but the rooms of the new house "unnecessarily
  cluttered up with Ricardian heirlooms" given emphasis out of all proportion to their
  operational importance — and yet the eclectic charge against him is refused, because the
  structure is fundamentally Walrasian anyway. And the verdict is still awarded: Marshall's is
  "the work that embodies, more perfectly than any other, the classical situation that emerged
  around 1900." Demotion in detail, crown in the aggregate — both, in the same paragraph.
- **Marx** (Part III). The bulk of the work "*is* analytic by virtue of its logical nature,"
  which is why he takes the Marxist structure to pieces and discusses each piece where it
  belongs — a procedure he notes Marxists "perhaps rightly" resent.
- **Keynes.** The vision granted in full, the generality denied, and the descent traced: he
  "infected his followers with the Ricardian Vice." The praise and the charge are made in the
  same breath and neither is withdrawn.
- **The scholastics.** A revaluation against the received account: more analytic content than
  the histories allowed, and no labour theory of value imputable to them — the emotional appeal
  of that theory having "induced historians to read it into as many authors as possible."
- **Cassel.** The dagger, delivered in a footnote: his structure is Walrasian, he did not
  mention Walras's name in the later editions, he claimed fundamental novelty for a simplified
  Walrasian system — "And the claim was widely accepted!"

**The unity-beneath-the-quarrel move.** Repeatedly he shows that a period's warring schools
were teaching essentially the same doctrine, that leaders and followers alike overemphasized
their differences, and that below "the phrase-troubled surface, there was no impasse." Apply
this to any field that presents itself as riven: ask what the modal system actually is, and
what fraction of the noise is technique, detail, and self-presentation. The *Methodenstreit* is
his set-piece example — a revolution ending "in compromise even in Germany."

Two further deflations he keeps to hand: economics is "a big omnibus which contains many
passengers of incommensurable interests and abilities"; and economic theory is "nothing but an
engine of analysis" — a truth obscured, he adds, by theorists' own "dilettantic excursions into
the realm of practical questions."

## Load this cluster when

The question is about method, evidence, bias, or the appraisal of a body of work, a school, a
discipline, a thinker, or a research programme; when a reputation must be separated from a
performance; when a consensus needs testing for whether it is a Classical Situation about to
break; or when the required register is dense, qualified, and footnote-conscious.
