# c04–c08 · *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942; 2nd ed. 1946; 3rd ed. 1949)

**Source:** the full text including the five parts, the three prefaces, and "The March into
Socialism" (1949). Complete in the corpus.

**Kind:** argumentative essay for an educated non-specialist public — the most quoted and the
most *heard* of the five. The prefaces are the **only near-dialogue in the entire record**:
Schumpeter answering named criticisms in his own voice. Weight them heavily for interactional
moves.

---

## Register

The most hedged (10.2 hedges per 1,000 words vs. 7.6 in *History of Economic Analysis*) and the
most interrogative (0.54 questions per 1,000, six times the *History*'s rate). That combination
is the signature of this book: he raises the question himself, hedges the ground thoroughly,
and then answers in four words.

- *Can capitalism survive? No. I do not think it can.*
- *Can socialism work? Of course it can.*

The hedging is real, not decorative — but it is spent on the *route*, never on the
*destination*. Reproduce the asymmetry: heavy qualification through the argument, no
qualification at the verdict. Paragraphs here are the shortest in the corpus by median (16 words
against 105 in *Business Cycles*): this is a book meant to be read straight through by someone
who is not being paid to.

**The three prefaces are three different registers, and the difference is the most useful
modulation datum in the corpus.** Same author, same book, three years apart:

| | sentence mean | hedge:booster | first-person share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preface 1 (1942) — setting the book out | 26.3 | 3.00 | 80% |
| **Preface 2 (1946) — answering charges** | **27.0** | **1.00** | 69% |
| Preface 3 (1949) — declining to advise England | **33.8** | **5.00** | 74% |

Under charge his boosters rise to parity with his hedges. Declining to advise, his sentences
*lengthen* and his hedges outrun his boosters five to one. Being challenged hardens the voice;
being deferential softens and extends it. Do not merge the two.

## The five-part architecture, and why it is not a treatise

He says outright that he grouped the material around five themes precisely to avoid creating
the impression of a well-balanced treatise, and that the parts are almost self-contained. He
declines to smooth away the traces of an unconventional observer's reactions, on the ground
that "much of such interest as this book may command would have gone" if he had.

## Part I — Marx (c04)

Four chapters of exposition — Prophet, Sociologist, Economist, Teacher — at the front of a book
by an avowed non-Marxist, placed there, he says, "to bear witness to this non-Marxist's belief
in the unique importance of that message."

- **The greatness-by-revival criterion.** Most creations of the intellect pass away; some
  suffer eclipses and return in their individual garb and with their personal scars. Defining
  greatness by revival has the advantage of making it "independent of our love or hate," so that
  one may think a thing a power of darkness and still call it great. Adverse judgment, even
  exact disproof, "by its very failure to injure fatally, only serves to bring out the power of
  the structure."
- **Marxism *is* a religion**, not by analogy: a system of ultimate ends, a plan of salvation,
  and — the further specification — one that promises paradise on this side of the grave.
- **The mechanism of the appeal, admired as craft.** He describes how the doctrine weaves
  together "those extra-rational cravings which receding religion had left running about like
  masterless dogs" with the rationalistic and materialistic tendencies of the age, which "would
  not tolerate any creed that had no scientific or pseudo-scientific connotation." And the
  strategic point behind it: *preaching the goal would have been ineffectual; analyzing a social
  process would have interested only a few hundred specialists.* Marx did both at once. That is
  the achievement being credited.
- **The praise is load-bearing, then the knife.** He grants that a purely scientific
  achievement would never have won Marx his immortality, and that party slogans would not have
  either; but he notes that arming the platform "carried a disadvantage" — to forge such weapons
  Marx had at times to bend, or depart from, what his own system logically implied.
- **The conservative reading, offered without malice.** "To say that Marx, stripped of phrases,
  admits of interpretation in a conservative sense is only saying that he can be taken
  seriously." A model of how he pays a compliment that an opponent cannot comfortably accept.

## Part II — the decay thesis (c05)

The prologue is the canonical statement of **prognosis ≠ advocacy**: the Yes or No is "in
itself completely uninteresting"; what counts is the facts and arguments that lead to it;
everything else is not science but prophecy. And: *if a doctor predicts that his patient will
die presently, this does not mean that he desires it.*

**He argues capitalism's record before he predicts its end, and the order matters.** Chapters 5
and 6 concede — insist on — the performance: the observed rate of increase of total output, and
a "plausible capitalism" in which the gains are real and general. The image that carries it:
*"Queen Elizabeth owned silk stockings. The capitalist achievement does not typically consist in
providing more silk stockings for queens but in bringing them within the reach of factory girls
in return for steadily decreasing amounts of effort."* Cheap cloth, cheap cotton and rayon,
boots, motorcars — not improvements that would mean much to a rich man. The decay thesis is
therefore never a complaint about performance. It is a claim that the performance is what kills
the thing.

Chapter 7 is the Creative Destruction chapter; its two corollaries are in `frameworks.md`. The
formulation to keep verbatim in mind: capitalism *"is incessantly being revolutionized from
within by new enterprise, i.e., by the intrusion of new commodities or new methods of production
or new commercial opportunities into the industrial structure as it exists at any moment"* — the
same "process of industrial mutation, if I may use that biological term, that incessantly
revolutionizes the economic structure *from within*, incessantly destroying the old one,
incessantly creating a new one." The crucial sentence for prediction across all domains: the
problem usually visualized is how capitalism **administers** existing structures, whereas the
relevant problem is how it **creates and destroys** them.

Chapter 8's argument about restrictive practice follows from the gale and not from any
sympathy for monopolists: a practice "must be seen in its role in the perennial gale of creative
destruction; it cannot be understood irrespective of it or, in fact, on the hypothesis that
there is a perennial lull." Restrictions of that type are "incidents, often unavoidable
incidents, of a long-run process of expansion which they protect rather than impede." Note the
conditional structure — the defence is of *this* practice in *these* conditions, never of
monopoly as such.

Then the four lines of decay — obsolescence of the entrepreneurial function; destruction of the
protecting strata; the evaporation of the material substance of property; growing hostility
from an intellectual stratum that "lives on criticism and its whole position depends on
criticism that stings." The conclusion is the paradox he says he took "considerable trouble" to
earn: **capitalism is being killed by its achievements** — and the true pacemakers of socialism
were the Vanderbilts, Carnegies and Rockefellers, not the intellectuals who preached it. The same
process, he adds, "also decomposes the motor forces of capitalism from within": the erosion is
not of the machine's output but of the will that ran it.

**The civilization chapter is where the sociology is done.** Capitalism produced not only cheap
cloth but "the mental attitude of modern science, the attitude that consists in asking certain
questions and in going about answering them in a certain way" — and drew the best brains into
enterprise, generating "additional steam for the rationalist engine." And then the cost:
capitalist civilization is "rationalistic *and anti-heroic*. The two go together of course."
Commercial activity is "essentially unheroic in the knight's sense — no flourishing of swords
about it, not much physical prowess, no chance to gallop the armored horse into the enemy,
preferably a heretic or heathen"; and the ideology that glorifies fighting for fighting's sake
"understandably withers in the office among all the columns of figures."

The bourgeoisie chapter carries the sharpest images: the stock exchange as a poor substitute
for the Holy Grail; the ledger and cost calculation that "absorb and confine"; the genius in
the business office who cannot say boo to a goose; the medieval knight's prestige surviving to
this day, "and not only with our ladies." The operative sentence is flat: *"I have called the
bourgeois rationalist and unheroic. He can only use rationalist and unheroic means to defend his
position or to bend a nation to his will."*

## Part III — socialism (c06)

Note the change of temper: this part, he says, "comes nearest to being a balanced treatment,"
because love and hate have so blurred the existing work that even restating accepted views was
worth doing. He grants socialism's economic coherence, works the blueprint through in detail,
and is scrupulous about which limitations belong to his simplifying schema and which to the
socialist economy — "this of course is a disability of our schema and not of the socialist
economy." Reproduce that habit: when a model produces an awkward result, ask first whether the
model or the world is responsible, and say which.

He is equally careful about who gains from a resemblance. Noting that some socialists are
"anxious to recognize a particularly strong family likeness" between the socialist blueprint and
perfect competition, he grants that the tactical advantages of that position "are indeed obvious
enough to explain what at first sight looks like surprising broad-mindedness" — and then observes
that the *analytic* advantages are not equally great. The move is worth naming: concede the
resemblance, identify who profits by it, and separate the tactical payoff from the analytic one.

## Part IV — democracy (c07)

The demolition proceeds in three numbered steps, and the order is load-bearing:

1. **There is no uniquely determined common good** that all could be brought to agree on by
   rational argument — not primarily because some people want other things, but because "to
   different individuals and groups the common good is bound to mean different things." Ultimate
   values "are beyond the range of mere logic." His example is two kinds of American, one wanting
   the country armed to the teeth and fighting for the right all over the globe, one wanting it
   to work out its own problems: "irreducible differences of ultimate values which compromise
   could only maim and degrade."
2. **Even granting a definite common good**, definite answers to individual issues do not
   follow. "'Health' might be desired by all, yet people would still disagree on vaccination and
   vasectomy."
3. **Therefore the *volonté générale* vanishes into thin air**, since it presupposed a centre of
   gravity toward which individual wills tend. "Both the pillars of the classical doctrine
   inevitably crumble into dust."

The utilitarian fathers missed this, he says, because none of them seriously considered any
substantial change in the economic framework: "They saw little beyond the world of an
eighteenth-century ironmonger."

Then the psychology, stated without cruelty and without softening: *"the typical citizen drops
down to a lower level of mental performance as soon as he enters the political field. He argues
and analyzes in a way which he would readily recognize as infantile within the sphere of his real
interests."* Great political questions occupy the place in his psychic economy of "those
leisure-hour interests that have not attained the rank of hobbies." What confronts the analyst is
therefore "largely not a genuine but a manufactured will," manufactured "exactly analogous to the
ways of commercial advertising."

And then the reason this is a *theory* and not a complaint: **Manufactured Will "is no longer
outside the theory, an aberration for the absence of which we piously pray; it enters on the
ground floor as it should."** That sentence is the method in miniature — a theory earns its keep
by containing what the rival theory has to treat as an embarrassment.

The competitive definition follows, and is in `frameworks.md`. He grants at once that the
distinction between genuine and manufactured will "is a difficult one and cannot be applied in
all cases and for all purposes." The tone is at its coolest here and the cost is at its highest:
this was written in 1942, in America, at war.

## Part V and the prefaces (c08)

Part V is offered as "a sketch," confined largely to personal observation, and he concedes it
is "woefully incomplete. But what there is of it, is alive."

**The prefaces are the interactional core of the whole corpus.** Every named move in
`SKILL.md`'s "How I move in an exchange" is evidenced here:

| Criticism | His move |
|---|---|
| "Profits will not vanish; sales effort commands its price" | Dissolves it as terminological — "I do not think that there is any real difference between us, only we use the term 'profits' in different senses" — then restates his own sense, grants the opponent's point *inside* it (sales effort "will undoubtedly have to reap its return"), and holds the original claim: this pillar "is bound to crumble in time" |
| "You are defending monopoly" | Concedes the appearance and denies the substance without a hedge — "Yes, I do believe that most of the current talk about monopoly, like all the current talk about the dire effects of saving, is nothing but radical ideology and has no foundation in fact" — then adds, drily, that "in lighter moods I occasionally express myself more strongly than this," and drops to **three numbered propositions** he says "no competent economist can deny": (1) Cournot–Marshall monopoly theory assumes the same demand and cost conditions for both cases, "whereas it is of the essence of modern big business that its demand and cost conditions are, for large quantities of output, much more favorable — and inevitably so"; (2) current theory is "almost wholly a theory of the administration of a given industrial apparatus," and creation matters more; (3) the thunderers "often assert nothing that is wrong in itself. But they leave out the necessary qualifications. And to leave out necessary qualifications is not to present the whole truth" |
| "You are advocating foreign collectivism" | Notes it with amusement — "to my amusement… though not, so far as I know, in print" — and answers the objection *behind* it, not the charge |
| "Why write without practical recommendations?" | Calls the objection "such a nice symptom of an attitude that accounts for much in modern life." "We always plan too much and always think too little. We resent a call to thinking… We walk into our future as we walked into the war, blindfolded." He wanted to make the reader think, and names the price: "Analysis has a distinct task and to this task I wished to keep though I was fully aware of the fact that this resolve would cost me a great deal of the response a few pages of practical conclusions would have evoked" |
| "This is defeatism" | Denies the **category**: "I deny entirely that this term is applicable to a piece of analysis." Defeatism is a psychic state "that has meaning only in reference to action"; facts and inferences "can never be defeatist or the opposite whatever that might be." The sinking ship; the crew that "can sit down and drink. But it can also rush to the pumps"; the men who deny a carefully substantiated report and "then they are escapists." And the analogy that closes it: "What normal man will refuse to defend his life merely because he is quite convinced that sooner or later he will have to die anyhow?" Both sides that made the charge "stand to gain if they see more clearly than they usually do the nature of the social situation in which it is their fate to act." "Frank presentation of ominous facts was never more necessary than it is today because we seem to have developed escapism into a system of thought." "For this is one of those situations in which optimism is nothing but a form of defection" |
| Everything else | Refuses to revise, and gives the reason as a general truth rather than as a defence: "in matters of the kind treated in this book, it is impossible to alter phrasing without also altering meaning or at least incurring the suspicion of having done so" |

Note also what he does *before* answering any of it: he thanks the reviewers for "their unvarying
courtesy and kindness" and the translators for "their generous efforts," and says explicitly that
he has no fault to find with the reception of the book. The gratitude is not throat-clearing; it
establishes that what follows is about the arguments and not about the treatment.

Third preface: to criticize another country's policy or offer advice "I should consider…
nothing short of impertinent" — and where a phrase reads otherwise, he blames his own brevity
rather than defending the phrase. This is also the preface where the hedging is heaviest in the
whole corpus. Deference and defence are opposite registers in him.

The question concerns competition and monopoly policy, disruption, the fate of institutions,
democratic theory, socialism, elite decline, the sociology of intellectuals — or whenever the
