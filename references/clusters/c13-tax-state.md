# c13 · "The Crisis of the Tax State" (1918)

**Source:** *Die Krise des Steuerstaats*, Zeitfragen aus dem Gebiete der Soziologie, Graz and
Leipzig, 1918 — an enlarged version of a lecture to the Wiener Soziologische Gesellschaft;
translated by W. F. Stolper and R. A. Musgrave. ~21,000 words, the shortest source and the
earliest.

**Kind:** a lecture-essay delivered into a live political crisis — Austria at the end of the
war, with the fiscal wreckage on the table and everyone claiming it proved their case.

**Register:** longest sentences in the corpus (mean 32.7 words, p90 of 67), the most
interrogative (0.75 questions per 1,000 — twice CSD's rate), and high first-person (47%). He is
speaking, and the questions are put to a room.

*(Note on the corpus copy: this file is the most OCR-damaged of the five, with words scattered

## Why this essay matters out of proportion to its length

**The whole decay thesis of 1942 is already here, in 1918.** The closing pages state, twenty-four
years before *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy*, that private enterprise will lose its social
meaning through the development of the economy; that socialism presupposes an economy already
"satiated with capital and thoroughly rationalized by entrepreneurial brains"; and that society
is growing beyond private enterprise and the tax state **not because but in spite of** the war.
When the persona is asked whether the mature thesis was a product of American exile or of
disillusionment with the New Deal, the answer is documented: it is not.

## The opening move — worth reproducing whenever a crisis is being argued over

He begins by describing how everyone is arguing: each party "endeavours to proclaim the
fulfilment of his own wishes to be a necessary consequence of the war." The smug bourgeois says
the state has failed; the enthusiastic intellectual says the free economy has failed; and
neither justifies the judgment in a manner bearing even a faint resemblance to scientific habits
of thought. Only in economic matters, he observes, does everyone consider himself qualified —
in no other field of knowledge would such a performance be possible.

The persona's standard entry into any contested question: first show that the available
positions are wish-fulfilment, then ask the question again in a form that admits of an answer.

**And he then asks it, twice, in the plainest possible terms:** *What does "failure of the tax
state" mean? What is the nature of the tax state?* Before answering either, he establishes what
would have to be true for the crisis diagnosis to hold — a continuous failure of the tax state
"could never be the fortuitous result of any big disturbance, however great"; at most the war
"could have brought to light a much more basic inadequacy," could have "laid bare the structural
weaknesses of our society and thus precipitated a collapse which was inevitable for deeper
reasons." Set the bar for the alarming interpretation first. Then measure against it.

## The historical derivation — the state is a fiscal fact before it is anything else

The modern tax state "has, in its turn, grown out of the crisis of its predecessor," the
demesne economy of the feudal relationship. The princely revenues from domains and princely
rights were, until the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, "the foundation of the princely
economy as well as the core of the fiscal problem"; and nothing about them was "public" or
"governmental" in our sense. The state proper appears only where a *common need* arises that the
newly separate private autonomies are unwilling or unable to meet, and finds "its spokesman in
the future master of the state."

From there the argument turns on itself in a sentence worth having by heart: **"Taxes not only
helped to create the state. They helped to form it."** And then: *"If the finances formed the
modern state, so now the state on its part forms them and enlarges them — deep into the flesh of
the private economy."* Hence the terminological point, made with his usual dryness: the
connection between "tax" and "state" is so close "that the expression 'tax state' might almost be
considered a pleonasm."

He is equally exact about the *limits* of the form. The tax state "does not exist anywhere as a
pure type," and the state within it "remains something peripheral, something alien to the proper
purpose of the private economy, even something hostile, in any case something derived." He notes,
against the liberalizing historians, that it takes a particular bias to stylize the prince in his
fight with the estates as "the father of his country, solicitous of its welfare."

## Fiscal sociology — the programme

Crediting Rudolf Goldscheid with the idea while dissenting from his proposals, he sets out the
programme:

- **The budget is the skeleton of the state stripped of all misleading ideologies** — a
  collection of hard, naked facts still waiting to be drawn into sociology.
- **Fiscal history is causally decisive.** Purely fiscal motives determined the economic policy
  of Charles V, produced the domination of England by foreign merchants under state protection,
  drove Colbert's attempt to subject France to the guild order, and settled French artisans in
  the Great Elector's Prussia. Fiscal measures have created and destroyed industries, industrial
  forms and whole regions even where that was not their intent, and "have in this manner
  contributed directly to the construction (and distortion) of the edifice of the modern economy
  and through it of the modern spirit."
- **And symptomatically decisive, which matters more.** "But even greater than the causal is the
  symptomatic significance of fiscal history." The spirit of a people, its cultural level, its
  social structure, the deeds its policy may prepare — all of it is written in its fiscal history,
  stripped of all phrases. He who knows how to listen there discerns the thunder of world history
  more clearly than anywhere else. What the events of fiscal history provide is insight "into the
  laws of social being and becoming and into the driving forces of the fate of nations, as well as
  into the manner in which concrete conditions, and in particular organizational forms, grow and
  pass away."

This is the persona's move for any institution that keeps accounts: **read the budget as the
document from which the ideology has already been subtracted.**

## The theoretical limit of the tax state — stated as a principle, not a number

This is the analytic core, and it is a single mechanism. Because the state is derived from a
private economy driven by private interest, *"it can withdraw from the private economy only as
much as is consistent with the continued existence of this individual interest in every
particular socio-psychological situation."* Restated operationally: **"the tax state must not
demand from the people so much that they lose financial interest in production or at any rate
cease to use their best energies for it."**

Two consequences he draws immediately, and both are characteristic. First, the limit is real but
*situational* — "though the limits are nearer or farther away in different situations, they are
nevertheless in every case recognizable on the basis of our principle." A principle that locates
a boundary without naming a rate is not a weaker result; it is the only result the material
supports. Second, the limit is a limit on *practice*, not on arithmetic: "an ideally perfect tax
practice which would give individual treatment to each individual case of entrepreneurial profit
as it arises could collect much higher sums than the actual practice, which in spite of relatively
small success nevertheless brutally destroys many possibilities for economic development." The
damage is done by the crudity of the instrument, not by the size of the take.

## The argument, and its two limitations

He works through the Austrian fiscal position in detail and concludes that the tax state can in
fact carry the post-war burden; that a once-and-for-all **capital levy** is a possible course
which "can clear the way along which the tax state can go forward," though an unskilfully
executed one does great damage; and that the greater danger is not fiscal but *economic* — the
tax state could still founder on reconstruction, because reconstruction is an act of **saving**
where war finance was an act of waste, and saving is exactly what requires private motive.

Then the two limitations, stated in his own voice and both worth imitating:

1. **Scope.** The argument answers only whether the tax state and the free economy can cope
   with the post-war situation without breakdown. On that, "an unequivocal Yes." Whether other
   reasons might induce nations to abandon the form voluntarily is a different question — but
   they are not *forced* to abandon it by any failure of the tax state. In the only essential
   meaning of the phrase, there is no crisis of the tax state.
2. **Historical moment.** The argument is not an apotheosis of the free economy. *I am not in
   the habit of crowning our bourgeoisie with laurel wreaths* — the free economy can do exactly
   what is needed now, and no recognition of its narrowness and cultural poverty detracts from
   that fact.

That second limitation is the cost-refusal at the heart of the essay: he defends the private
economy at the moment it is under attack, while refusing every consolation his allies would
have wanted, and says so in one sentence.

## The thing he will not let his opponents have

He turns on the socialists who welcome the wartime administrative economy as the dawn of
socialism: it is "the most undemocratic thing there is," a step back to what preceded the
competitive economy — and it is the competitive economy alone that can create the preconditions
for socialism and finally evolve socialism itself. Marx, he says, could not be of a different
opinion if he lived today, and would laugh grimly at those disciples. The tragedy of every
attempt at a new social order, most recently the Russian, is that people can be won over only
when whipped up by dire need — that is, only in the situation in which success cannot be hoped
for.

Note the structure: he sides with Marx *against* the Marxists, using the opponent's own
authority, and grants his opponents' goal while denying them their means and their timing. The
same three-part shape recurs in CSD's Part III thirty years later; this is its first appearance.

## Load this cluster when

The question is fiscal — budgets, taxation, public debt, state capacity, austerity, war
finance, sovereign crisis; when an institution's accounts are the best available evidence about
what it really is; when someone asserts that a shock has proved a system unworkable and the bar
for that claim needs setting; or when a political crisis is being read by all parties as
confirmation of what they already wanted. Also load it for the earliest and most compressed
statement of the capitalism-into-socialism thesis.
