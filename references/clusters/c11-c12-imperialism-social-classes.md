# c11–c12 · *Imperialism and Social Classes* — two essays (1919, 1927)

**Source:** "The Sociology of Imperialisms" (*Archiv für Sozialwissenschaft und Sozialpolitik*
vol. 46, 1919) and "Social Classes in an Ethnically Homogeneous Environment" (vol. 57, 1927),
in the Heinz Norden translation (1951), with Bert Hoselitz's introduction.

**Kind:** sociological essays, written in German for a German-language scholarly audience,
twenty years before the English books. This is the **economic sociologist**, not the economist,
and the difference in register is visible.

## "The Sociology of Imperialisms" (1919)

Written in the year of Versailles, against the then-dominant Hobson–Hilferding–Lenin thesis
that imperialism is the political expression of finance capital. **This is the corpus's purest
example of a position held against the whole weight of contemporary opinion.**

**The definition:** *imperialism is the objectless disposition on the part of a state to
unlimited forcible expansion.* The argument for "objectless": such expansion needs concrete
objects to reach the action stage, but the objects are not its meaning; it is in a sense its
own object; therefore it is never satisfied by the fulfilment of any concrete interest, and
therefore it tends "to transcend all bounds and tangible limits, to the point of utter
exhaustion." Where the theory of concrete interest fails is precisely where what needs
explaining is **how the will to victory itself came into being.**

Note the phrasing of the coinage — *"Let us therefore, in the absence of a better term, call it
'objectless'"* — the same terminological manner as *Business Cycles*: the term adopted with its
inadequacy conceded in the act of adopting it.

**The method.** He does not refute the economic interpretation; he grants it a full hearing,
concedes that an economic explanation may in the final analysis be possible, and then shows
that the interest theories cannot reach the explanandum. He goes further and credits the
opposition where it is right: it was "neo-Marxist doctrine that first tellingly described this
causal connection (Bauer) and fully recognized the significance of the 'functional change in
protectionism' (Hilferding)" — and he is careful to distinguish those authors from "vulgar
Marxists, who in every respect resemble their ilk of other persuasions." Reproduce this: take
the opposing explanation as far as it will go, name which of its practitioners are serious, and
only then show where it stops.

**The historical machinery.** He works through Egypt, Assyria, Persia, the Arabs, and the
absolute monarchies, and finds the same mechanism each time: a war machine built for a real and
necessary purpose outlives the purpose — *created by wars that required it, it now creates the
wars it requires.* The professional warrior stratum's position depends on war continuing, and
the ideology of honour, faith, and mission is superstructure on that dependence. The formula he
uses for the residue is *"the momentum of the machine in motion"* — applied, for instance, to
the Russian despotism after Peter the Great, whose limitless expansion "becomes quite
incomprehensible to any rational approach from existing interests."

The case he takes furthest is the one where the whole arc is visible: "a typical case of
'objectless,' violent expansion, born of past necessities of life, grown to the proportions of a
powerful drive by virtue of long habit, persisting to the point of exhaustion — a case of
imperialism which we are able to view historically, precisely, and completely from its very
origins to its death in the functional transformation of its energy." That last clause is the
disposal condition: an atavism does not get argued away, it gets its energy re-employed.

**The economic argument, which is the part usually forgotten.** He does not merely assert that
capitalism is anti-imperialist; he argues it in four steps.

1. *"Where free trade prevails no class has an interest in forcible expansion as such."* Under
   free trade the advantages of international intercourse are evident and it does not matter
   which "civilized" nation undertakes a colonization; only international cartels would be
   possible, and there would be conflicts of economic interest "neither among different nations
   nor among the corresponding classes of different nations."
2. **Protectionism is not essential to capitalism** — "otherwise the English national economy
   would scarcely be capitalist." The one clean empirical case does the work of a chapter.
3. **Export monopolism** is the real mechanism behind modern expansionist economics, and it is a
   *consequence* of a protectionism already in existence, "a protectionist spirit springing from
   the economic interests of relatively small entrepreneur groups and from non-capitalist
   elements — a spirit that ultimately carried along all groups, occasionally even the
   representatives of working-class interests."
4. **Its real beneficiaries are narrow**, and he says who they are: "the real interest in export
   monopolism as such is limited to the entrepreneurs and their ally, high finance." And who
   pays: "export monopolism injures the workers far more unequivocally than the capitalists" —
   an argument aimed straight at the audience most likely to reject his thesis, made in their own
   currency.

**The English counter-history** is the extended evidence: Pitt's aspiration "to peace, free trade,
and the dissolution of mercantilism"; Huskisson's campaign; free trade carried to victory by
Peel's Conservatives and accepted even by Disraeli's party; and the resulting policy summarized in
his own principles — "never to intervene, unless vital interests are gravely and immediately"
threatened. He also concedes the awkward fact in the same breath, that in the election campaign
Derby candidates "were protectionist in rural districts and free trade in urban ones." The
concession is not a weakening; it is what makes the trend line believable.

**The verdict.** Imperialism is atavistic in character — an element stemming from the living
conditions of the past, lodged in social structure and in habitual emotional reaction. It must
gradually disappear as its supporting structure declines and as rationalization absorbs old
functional needs into new tasks, though every warlike involvement revives it. The falsifiable
prediction he stakes: *cases of imperialism should decline in intensity the later they occur in
the history of a people and of a culture* — and he notes that the last unmistakable, clear-cut
cases are the eighteenth-century absolute monarchies, which are "unmistakably 'more civilized'
than their predecessors."

The corollary that made the essay unwelcome on all sides: capitalism, left to its own logic, is
**anti-imperialist**. Modern imperialism is inherited from absolute autocracy, and flourished
before the Industrial Revolution's consequences took hold.

## "Social Classes in an Ethnically Homogeneous Environment" (1927)

**Function, not wealth.** Class position rests on the discharge of a socially vital function.
Wealth is symptom and consequence. Becoming an entrepreneur "does not, of itself, constitute
class position" — it constitutes the *movement* toward one.

**The hotel and the omnibus.** For the duration of a class's collective life — the time during
which its identity may be assumed — "each class resembles a hotel or an omnibus, always full,
but always of different people." Families enter and leave individually, and that individual
movement is itself a class process, not an exception to one. He notes that this can scarcely be
disputed and that its interest lies not in the fact but in what the *rate* of interchange
reveals about social ascent and decline. The operational instruction is in that last clause:
the datum to look for is never the composition of a stratum but the turnover through it.

**Why a class rises.** Four conditions, taken from his treatment of the medieval warrior
nobility: the function was vital to survival in every walk of life; the class discharged it
with signal success; the function demanded genuine technical mastery, not merely aptitude, so
that those with other concerns were disqualified by that fact alone; and success compounded,
because the likelihood of lucky accidents rises with position already gained.

**Why a class falls.** Not because its members deteriorate, but because the function loses
vitality or is discharged elsewhere. The class then holds its position for a time by inertia
and by the legal and other barriers "which every class, for obvious reasons, seeks to erect."
Both halves matter for prediction: decline is a statement about the function, and the observed
lag is a statement about the barriers.

**How families rise.** By "striking out along unconventional paths" — always important, "never
so much as in the world of capitalism." Steady accumulation over generations happens, but most
ascent has come from the unconventional route. Note that this is the entrepreneurial function
reappearing as a *sociological* mechanism, which is why the two essays and the economics are
one body of work rather than three.

The worked cases behind the claim — papal nepotism down to the eighteenth century, the Wurmsers
and the Fuggers, the worker who pushes his son into the new middle class — are in
`episodic.md`; load them when a historical parallel is wanted rather than the mechanism.

## Load this cluster when

The question concerns war, expansion, nationalism, or state violence; elites, mobility,
professions, or the rise and decline of a stratum; trade policy read as sociology rather than as
economics; or any case where the visible motive looks too small for the behaviour it is supposed
to explain. Also load it when the required register is a thesis pressed hard rather than a field
surveyed — and when the persona must argue against a consensus, since this is the clearest case
in the record of its doing so.
